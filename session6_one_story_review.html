The one analogy we'll use all the way through

The network is a postal system.

  • A building is a local network: one Wi-Fi, one office floor, one switch.
  • Every device wears a permanent badge printed at the factory: its MAC address.
  • Inside the building a mail cart delivers by badge number: the switch.
  • A street address is an IP address: the building's street (the network part) plus the apartment (the host part).
  • A post office sits between buildings and sorts mail by street: the router. Its sorting table is the routing table.
  • Post offices learn their sorting tables by talking to each other: routing protocols.
  • The phone book turns a name into a street address: DNS.
  • Registered mail with receipts is TCP. Postcards are UDP. The apartment number on the envelope is the port.

Every tab adds one piece to this picture and says why the picture needed it. The widgets are smaller than usual and there are more of them. The point today is the connections, not any one mechanism.

The cable: how bits become a delivery

The question this layer answers: how do two devices on the same cable or Wi-Fi hand each other data, and how do they know which device it's for?

Bits on a wire

An Ethernet cable, or a Wi-Fi radio, carries ones and zeros as voltage changes or radio pulses. The cable knows nothing about computers, addresses, or web pages. It carries bits, in order, from one end to the other. That's all a physical layer is.

Giving the bits a shape: the frame

Bits with no agreed shape are noise. So both ends agree on a format, the frame: first the destination's badge number, then the sender's, then a tag saying what's inside, then the contents, then a checksum so a damaged frame can be thrown away. The badge numbers are MAC addresses, printed into every network card at the factory, unique in the world, never changing.

How devices identify each other

Every network card does one check on every frame it sees: is the destination badge mine? If yes, keep it and pass it up. If no, drop it. That's it. There is no "login," no lookup. On a shared medium like Wi-Fi, every device hears every frame and most of them throw most of it away. The special badge ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff means "everyone keep this," and that's a broadcast.

The mail cart and the question to the room

With more than two devices you need something in the middle. The switch is a mail cart that learns which badge sits on which cable by reading the "From" of every frame it carries, and delivers by reading the "To." It never opens the envelope. If it hasn't seen a badge yet, it shows the frame to every cable and lets the right device keep it.

One gap remains: your program knows the other device by its street address (IP), not its badge. So before the first frame, the sender asks the whole building: "who has this address? tell me your badge." That's ARP, and the answer goes into a small list so the question isn't asked again for a while. Everything in this tab is layer 2: delivery inside one building, by badge.

Why IP sits on top of MAC

The question this layer answers: how do you reach a device that is not in your building, when all you can do is hand frames to badges in your own building?

Why badges aren't enough

A MAC address says which device, but nothing about where it is. Two badges one digit apart can be on opposite sides of the planet. You cannot sort the world's mail by badge number: a post office would need a list of every badge on earth and where it is right now, updated every time a laptop moves. Nobody can hold that list.

What IP adds

An IP address is assigned when a device joins a network, and it has structure: the first part says which network (the street), the rest says which device on it (the apartment). The subnet mask says where the split is. Now sorting is possible: a post office doesn't need to know every apartment, only which direction each street is. That's the whole reason for a second address. MAC is who you are; IP is where you are today.

The decision every sender makes

Before writing the envelope, a device compares the destination's street to its own. Same street: the destination is in this building, so ARP for its badge and hand the frame straight to it. Different street: the destination is somewhere else, so hand the frame to the post office in this building, the default gateway, and let it sort from there. Either way the IP envelope says the real destination. Only the badge on the outer envelope changes: the neighbor's, or the post office's.

Where switching and routing come in

The question this tab answers: what do the two boxes in the middle actually do, and why are they different boxes?

Two tables, two jobs

Both the mail cart and the post office work from a table. The tables look similar and do completely different things.

  • The switch's table maps badge to cable: "badge …0b sits on cable 2." It fills itself by watching frames go by. It's about this building only, and it never looks past the outer envelope.
  • The router's table maps street to door: "anything on 10.2.0.x goes out door 2." It's filled by hand or by routing protocols. The router opens the outer envelope, reads the street on the IP envelope, picks the door, and writes a new outer envelope for the next building.

That's the whole difference. A switch forwards by badge inside one building and changes nothing. A router forwards by street between buildings and replaces the badge envelope every time, lowering the TTL counter by one so a lost letter can't circle forever. When several rows in the sorting table match a street, the most specific row wins (the longest prefix), which is also why the default route, "everything else," only catches what nothing else did.

Switching inside, routing between

So "switching" is delivery within a building by badge, and "routing" is delivery between buildings by street. A VLAN is drawing walls inside one building so one cart serves two separate floors that can't hear each other. A layer 3 switch is a cart that also has a sorting table, so it can be the post office between its own floors. None of this is a new idea; it's the two tables placed in different boxes.

How the sorting tables get filled

The question this tab answers: a post office needs a row for every street in the world. Who writes those rows, and how does it find the best door?

Three ways a row gets into the table

  1. It's on your doorstep. The streets attached to the post office's own doors go in automatically. Connected routes.
  2. Somebody typed it. Fine for a village post office with one road out: "everything goes that way." That's the default route, and every laptop has one. It breaks when there are many roads, because nobody can type the world, and because a typed row doesn't know when a road is closed.
  3. The post offices tell each other. This is a routing protocol, and there are two ways to do it.

OSPF: the regional post offices share one map

Inside one organization, every post office does three things. Discovery: it says hello out of every door, and the post offices that answer become its neighbors; the hellos keep going so a dead road is noticed in seconds. Sharing: it describes its own roads, each with a cost, and that description is passed along until every post office in the region has every description. Now they all hold the same map. Choosing: each one computes the cheapest route from itself to every street and writes the results into its sorting table.

The cost is the important detail. It isn't the number of post offices on the way; it's a number per road that comes from the road's speed. A fast ten-lane highway costs 1; a slow country lane costs 10. So OSPF will happily send a letter through three fast post offices rather than one slow road. Older protocols counted hops, and they would pick the slow road. When a road closes, the two post offices on it update their descriptions, the news spreads, everyone recomputes, and the tables are right again within seconds, with no human involved.

BGP: the world's post offices trade promises

Between organizations nobody shares a map; nobody would. Instead each post office tells its neighbors "I can deliver to these streets," and attaches the list of organizations the promise has already passed through. Each hop adds its own name to the front. Discovery here is deliberate: two post offices are configured to talk to each other, over a TCP connection, and they stay in touch. Building the table: a post office collects the promises it hears and keeps the best one per street. Choosing: its own policy first (a cheaper or preferred neighbor wins), then the shortest list of organizations. There is no map and no cost per road, because nobody can see inside anyone else's region. When a promise is withdrawn, the news ripples out the same way, over minutes rather than seconds.

Delivery: where TCP and UDP come in

The question this layer answers: the letter has reached the right building and the right device. Which program gets it, and did all of it arrive?

The apartment number

One device runs many programs. IP got the letter to the building; something has to say which apartment. That's the port, a number on the transport envelope: 80 is the web server, 443 the secure web server, 53 the DNS server, 22 SSH. The sender picks a random high number for itself so the reply knows which of its programs to come back to. This is layer 4, and both TCP and UDP carry ports. They differ in everything else.

Registered mail: TCP

IP delivers each packet separately and promises nothing: packets can arrive out of order, twice, or not at all, because every post office along the way is allowed to drop one when it's busy. TCP turns that into something you can build a web page on. Before any data: a three-message handshake, "can you hear me, yes can you hear me, yes," so both sides know the other is there. Then every packet is numbered, every packet gets a receipt, and anything without a receipt is sent again. The receiver puts the numbered pieces back in order. Slower, with more envelopes, and nothing is ever lost.

Postcards: UDP

No handshake, no numbers, no receipts, no resending. Send it and move on. For a video call or a game, a late packet is useless, so stopping the whole stream to resend it would only add lag; UDP drops it and keeps going. For DNS, the question is one packet and the answer is one packet, so a handshake would triple the cost; ask again if nothing comes back. The choice is always "what does this program do when a packet is late?"

And the two helpers above it

Two more pieces finish the picture and both are just letters sent this way. DNS is the phone book: you know a name, you need a street address before you can write any envelope at all, so you send one UDP postcard to a resolver and get one back. DHCP is the front desk: when you walk into the building you have only your badge, and it hands you a street address, the mask, the post office's address, and the phone book's address. Then HTTP is the actual letter the browser writes, inside registered mail, to apartment 80. HTTPS is the same letter in a locked box.

The story, in order

This is the order the network uses, and the order you tell it in. Each line names the piece and the reason it's there.

  1. Bits on a cable or radio carry nothing by themselves, so devices agree on a frame with a destination and source MAC. A card keeps a frame when the badge is its own.
  2. With more than two devices, a switch learns badge to cable and delivers inside the building. ARP turns the IP a program knows into the MAC a frame needs.
  3. Badges can't be sorted by place, so every device also gets an IP address with a network part and a host part. The mask says where the split is, and the sender uses it to decide near or far.
  4. Far letters go to the router, which opens the badge envelope, reads the street on the IP envelope, picks a door from its sorting table by the most specific match, lowers TTL, and writes a new badge envelope for the next building.
  5. Sorting tables are filled by connected streets, static rows for villages, and routing protocols for everything else: OSPF shares one map in a region and picks the cheapest route by road speed; BGP trades promises between regions and picks by policy then shortest path.
  6. At the destination device the port picks the program. TCP adds a handshake, numbering, and receipts so nothing is lost; UDP sends postcards when late data is useless.
  7. Before any of it: DHCP handed the device its address, mask, gateway, and resolver; DNS turned the name into an address. On top of all of it: HTTP is the letter, HTTPS the locked box.

Practice

These are all "why" questions. Answer each in two or three sentences, out loud.

1. Why does a frame need a destination MAC if the cable only goes to one place?

Because cables usually don't: Wi-Fi and switched networks have many devices hearing or receiving frames. The badge is how each card decides whether a frame is its own.

2. Why can't a switch deliver to another city?

It only knows badges on its own cables, and badges carry no location. Reaching another city needs an address that groups by place, which is IP, and a device that sorts by it, which is a router.

3. Why does the router rewrite the Ethernet envelope but not the IP one?

The Ethernet envelope is for one building; the next building has different badges. The IP envelope names the final destination, which doesn't change until it's delivered.

4. Why do routers need a protocol when switches don't?

A switch learns everything it needs by watching the frames in its own building. A router needs to know about streets it has never seen, in buildings far away, and the only way to learn that is for routers to tell each other.

5. Why "cost" and not "hops" in OSPF?

Hops ignore speed. Three fast roads can deliver faster than one slow one. Cost is set from road speed so the cheapest total is the fastest path, not the shortest-looking one.

6. Why doesn't BGP use costs?

It can't see the roads inside other organizations. All it hears is "I can deliver there, via these organizations." So it picks by its own policy, then by the fewest organizations in the path.

7. Why does TCP live only on the two devices and not in the routers?

Reliability is a promise between the sender and the receiver. The post offices in between just carry letters and are allowed to drop them; the receipts and resending happen at the ends.

8. Why does DNS happen before the TCP handshake?

The handshake needs an IP address to send the SYN to. The name has to become an address first, and that's one postcard to the resolver.
